Why halal certification is becoming a growth lever for African SMEs

12 June 2026 · 6 min read · By Zainab Kimani

For years, halal certification was treated by most small food producers as a box to tick before they could sell into a particular market. It cost money, it took time, and beyond that, it rarely came up in conversation. That is changing quickly, and the businesses that noticed first are now several steps ahead of everyone else on the exhibition floor.

At the 2026 edition of the Ishmar Halal Trade Expo, we asked buyers what made them stop at a stand versus walk past it. Certification came up unprompted in nearly two-thirds of the conversations. Not price. Not packaging. Certification.

The buyer's problem, not just the seller's

Distributors sourcing for supermarkets in Nairobi, Kampala or Dubai are not halal certification experts. They are trying to move quickly through a hall of a few hundred exhibitors, and a visible, verifiable certification mark does something specific: it removes one entire category of due diligence they would otherwise have to do themselves.

That is the shift. Certification is no longer a claim you make about your product. It has become a shortcut you hand to the buyer, and buyers reward whoever makes their job easier.

"We closed three distribution deals on day two alone. The buyer quality at Ishmar is unlike any other trade show we've attended in the region." — Amina Yusuf, Export Manager, Zamzam Foods

What the data from our floor shows

We track badge scans against stand visits at every edition. Exhibitors with visible, current certification documentation displayed at the stand saw 40 percent more scan-to-conversation conversions than those without it prominently shown, even when product categories were similar.

That gap held steady across food, cosmetics and pharmaceuticals, the three sectors where certification carries the most weight with institutional buyers.

Three things exhibitors got right

  • They renewed certification well before the event, rather than scrambling in the final week.
  • They displayed the certificate itself, not just a logo, somewhere a buyer could actually read it.
  • They trained stand staff to answer certification questions confidently, without redirecting to "someone who handles that."

What this means for 2027

We are building a dedicated certification verification desk into next year's floor plan, so buyers can confirm a claim on the spot rather than taking it on trust. Exhibitors who get ahead of that now, by having documentation ready and staff briefed, will have one less thing to worry about in March.

Certification will not replace a good product. It never has. But at a trade show where buyers have perhaps four minutes for your stand, it is increasingly the difference between a real conversation and a polite nod on the way past.
